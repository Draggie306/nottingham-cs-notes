

### Why ethics matters in computing

- The profession is so widespread and wide-reaching on society
- Implications at a population level are difficult to measure - e.g. the use of social media from a young age
- Software and AI systems are present in any single sector in society - healthcare, hiring, policing, relationships, changing the way teaching is done
- Every design choice is also an ethical decision: who benefits from using this technology? How can software or hardware be designed and created that will benefit society more than it harms it?
- Harms are rarely intentional; instead, many people can be harmed because of a lack of budget, improper stakeholder analysis, and people not informed about changes

> Can technology familiarise ourselves with our own mortality but also help us live more?


### DeathTech
Is an umbrella term about technologies that assist and prepare for death. Examples include griefbots: (designed to help with grief and reassure users); deathbots; digital wills; digital remembrance platforms.

It is used across hospices, homes, hospitals and online. It can involve palliative care staff, intensive care units, grief therapists and friends and family. 

It can help a lot: 
- signposting to e.g. theraputic support services;
- comfort, accept, and give people new meanings for coping with loss;
- sharing of peer/other people's experiences and stories;

However:
- it can prolong and cause emotional dependencies, which could turn griefbots into a recurring subscription model;
- prolong grief, with rewarding of continued engagement with those who have died;
- there is a lack of consent and regulation;
- users can be manipulated more easily under a heightened state of vulnerability.
	- Combined with a lack of regulation, encouraging users to keep talking, and sycophantically validating users' feelings, this could be exploited

> These are generalisable: who should consent across software, how is risk managed to avoid dependency on technology, etc.


### Patterns of harm

1. Consent and autonomy: was the affected person ever asked and gave their approval?
2. Dignity and bias: who trained the system, does it exclude anyone?
3. Dependency: does it encourage and exploit vulnerable moments?
4. Governance lag: are regulation and policies up-to-date and informed about development and deployment?
5. Commercialisation: is a business built on distress, pain or vulnerability?
