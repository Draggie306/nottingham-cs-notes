## Presentation
Every project is allocated a 20-minute slot. There is a session "chair" - if you begin to run out of time (at 14-15 minutes), they may stand up and raise their hands. 

The structure is suggested to be:
- An inventive introduction lasting ~1 minute.
- Live presentation of 8-10 minutes - what has been done, why others should care, who did each part, and how it went.
- A live demo, lasting 3-5 minutes. 
- Q&A - 5 minutes maximum (fixed)

> It helps groups' marks to ask questions. 

Having large, bright writing is much better than small text psychologically. You could say something controversial/about something unusual/an interesting and intriguing fact about the project, and a surprise factor (number of things to process/scenarios that can be dealt with) are all great to attract attention. 

**Focus on a core message and takeaway - solving a problem in a healthcare setting - presenting it and filling in the details for the talk.** Keep it high-level - ignore the technical details, text editor you prefer. You may want to talk about e.g. a game engine, but even this is too technical. The audience should be considered technical, but not experts - without knowing a thing about the project. **This core message should guide what the rest of the presentation is about** - avoid unnecessary detours, side quests, digression. The audience will get lost and will lose attention.

The audience should be focused on. They will not have a clue about the project - no buzzwords; there may be 1 overall thing but be careful. Think about the context: the team knows lots about the context and who is good at certain things in the team. It is never a bad idea to err on the side of being caution and over-explaining, than to under-explain and lose people. 

Be concise and to-the-point, with clarity throughout. At the end, give a message and ideas that should resonate with the audience. It is much better to finish early than late - after 15 minutes, there will be an interruption. The team must practice to get the timing right.

Do not have enormous blocks of text on slides, and do not have too many slides. Do not have a bombardment of bullet points - not bad to structure slides, but this can bore the reader, they should be broken up with a picture or diagram.

Planning the talk with exactly who will talk when - it is easier to speak in 5-minute chunks with different people, but avoid too many speaker changes.

## Q&A 
The team should agree beforehand who answers subject questions. If there is an engine with a specialist developer, they should be the one to answer questions, and teammates who did other work should answer questions based on what they developed.

Members should take turns grilling the team and asking awkward with pushy questions to simulate what is going on. 

The team does not need to know everything, or if it does not come to you. **It is fine to say "I will get back to you afterwards"**. If there is a brutally hard question, it is nearly certain they have misunderstood - so asking them to rephrase is a good idea. 

## Preparing
Preparation is the strongest possible antidote to being nervous. Focus on the opening of the talk. Avoid memorising the talk - **every member of the team should be natural instead**.

Borrow from others: look to other presentations - industry/tech talk presentations online, with extras they deliver such as videos which can be incorporated into the team. Also consider what makes other presentations bad - think about why a lecturer is rubbish, and think about how *not* to do that. This can be spread: one member can spend a few hours to find good examples of tasks and what to include in each.
