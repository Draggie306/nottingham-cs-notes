## Control types

Discrete
- Activation - turning a machine on/off
- Data entry - when a certain value is assigned to something - e.g. entering a letter or a number
- Setting - switching to a specific machine state (aeroplane mode, silent mode)

Continuous
- Quantitative setting - setting the machine to a particular value turning a colour pallet across a gradient
- Continuous control - continuously altering machine state, e.g. a car steering wheel 

> Coursework 1: Design principles like feedback: when the system tells you what is happening, especially if something goes wrong. Can be visual, audio, tactile (vibrations)

### Factors in control design

- Feedback can come in many forms. E.g. AMBER alert: vibrations, lights, and loud noises.
- Resistance: can make the experience tougher (more barriers) if the user is not going in the "right way".
- Size: surface area depending on use case
- Texture: needed for grip, to prevent damage, rubber to add grip and for durability 
- Coding: value in highlighting different aspects or areas of an interface. Can be done by colour , sound, shape, size, texture, etc.
- Handedness: had to change design of a phone because left-handed people cover the antenna; grip strength and orientation
- Clothing and protective clothing: larger buttons if being used outdoors/gloves, a trackpad is difficult
- Movements required for different controls: 
	- **open** (not consistently in control: you press and let go) vs **closed chain operations** (a pen/mouse - you are always in control - but increases fatigue. A trade off between tradeoff/accuracy) 
	- Taps being sophisticated depending on culture
- Ambiguity of orientation - to turn left or right? Ensure there is no ambiguity

### Sequence of use principle
Time sequencing: what will the user journey look like on the page? The start, next, after that? For a payment or booking page/basket: lots of students go straight to payment -> address. After the basket, there should be a summary of it, to show and understand it, then show delivery dates. And then, ask for the address. Give the money at the end, ensure they understand everything, and then confirm it. 

Think about an assembly line: things are made one at a time.


### Functional sequence
Things that work similarly together. For example: all connectivity are together - wifi, bluetooth, airdrop - these are similar things according to function, so it is easier for the suer to access if they are all placed in one area if they are related. Supermarkets: all related items together; settings menus.

Do not overload the user with things to access: use time and function sequencing. 


### Frequency of use principle
Controls and displays of items should be arranged in terms of how frequently they are used.

e.g. right click: research performed, the most frequent operations required are given.

A caveat: even though some things may not be used frequently (alarms/emergency buttons), they should not be hidden away.
