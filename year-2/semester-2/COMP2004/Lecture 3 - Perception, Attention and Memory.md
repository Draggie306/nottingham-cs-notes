
Perception, attention and memory are building blocks of HCI. 

Components of human perception include: 
- Colour 
- Figure ground
- Feature analysis
- Top-down vs bottom-up processing
- Absolute vs relative discrimination
- 3D perception

> This is all about human cognition.

## Perception
Perception is the cognitive process by which sensations are organised into an inner representation of the world. 

- Detection - is a stimulus present? 
- Identification - what is the stimulus? Visual/audition
- Categorisation - what group does the stimulus belong to?


For HCI, there is a natural tendancy from left-right, top-bottom on a screen, with scanning behaviour. We look at things in a particular order - highlights, bigger elements will be focuses on but smaller elements will be ignored.

Wherever we are looking, there is high resolution. In the periphery, there is low resolution - we cannot tell much difference between colour, but we can pick up on **change and movement**. These are valuable when labelling and designing a layout, having implications on contrasts, sizes and colours.

### Colour 

Colour is a valuable tool to grab attention. Having good contrast is important, but even with contrast, colours may not be complementary: 

![Pasted image 20260205150900](../../../Images/Pasted%20image%2020260205150900.png)

Colour stereoscopy: light colours make rooms appear larger, a red background is perceived as closer, and blue is better for a background. Colours at opposite ends of the spectrum will focus on different parts of the retina. Red = bad, green = ”good” implication of an action.

Designs should be made for monochrome first, and colours added afterwards. Low levels of illumination should not use colour coding, particularly reds, whereas monochrome e.g. clock radios and baby monitors is good for low light. 

Using different types of stimuli helps users understand the same information more effectively.

> Stroop effect: demonstration of interference on reaction time on a task, e.g. reading colours from the colour vs labelled differently. 


## Figure ground
The visual system of a scene should be simplified into two parts:

- the main object we are looking at (figure)
- everything else (ground)

The brain unconsciously uses many cues, including colours, hue, form, size, shape.


## Feature analysis
Breaking down stimuli into component parts - features. 

Features are matched to stored patterns in long-term memory, and the best match determines what is perceived. It is a hierarchical process.

### Fonts
Sans-serif fonts are better than serif fonts to recognise, being simpler. Serifs can mix letters together 

Full words are always better than abbreviations: poor error messages using technical messages.


## Top-down & bottom-up processing 
People tend to process information in a top-down manner: 

- Top-down: trying to quickly understand the pattern and using memory to recognise, **based on experience without looking at the details.**
- Bottom-up: look for and extract features, build patterns, build perception, then understanding the meaning, **based on building up visual understanding by inspecting all details.** 

Designs should prioritise top-down processing for text/objects (use real words, restrict verbosity, providing context) and optimise bottom-up processing for text (ensuring contrast).

## Absolute vs relative discrimination

People are very good at relative judgements (e.g. labelling people based on height is easy) but poor at absolute judgements (working out the exact height). For design, the number of absolute judgements should be reduced to 7 levels, with 4 or fewer being optimal.


## 3D perception
Drawings that are made to look 3D often have converging lines, lighting and shading, relative/contrasting heights and sizes of objects, and background items being less detailed (aerial perspective).
