- Functionality - how accurate and suitable system is
- Reliability - how recoverable it is, and fault tolerance (even if the design is good, errors may happen)
- Usability - how easy it is to understand and to learn to use the system
- Efficiency - how many resources are being used to use the system (effort, time)
- Maintainability - how easy it is to change, analyse
- Portability - how easy it is to install somewhere else, to coexist with other systems

Usability depends on who the users are (their characteristics), what the users’ goals are (their needs) and where they carry out their tasks (environmental)


## Measuring usability
We can use several measures to analyse the usability of a design or implementation:

- Effectiveness - the extent to which a user’s goal is achieved, objectively. Analysed by the % of tasks completed, or the ratio of success to failure.
- Efficiency - the amount of effort required to accomplish a goal. Assessed by examining the time spent on a task, the workload (physical/mental/cognitive - too difficult to work out how to do something mentally), deviation from the critical path, error rates
- Satisfaction - comfort and acceptability of the solution, subjectively. It measures the “ease of use”, how users rate its utility of functionality, and user likes/dislikes.


## Higher-order usability components

- Guessability - particularly for lesser-used items e.g. a fire extinguisher. It should be intuitive to learn, at a glance.
- Learnability - how fast to reach a reasonable level of understanding
- How fast/how many steps it will take a proficient user to execute a specific task.
- System potential - the maximum level of performance or users already proficient in using it.
- Reusability - for systems that are not used often. Reusable means the user does not need to re-read the documentation all over again.

The more labels you need, the less usable a system is.
